# ORIENTATION

## Introduction: How to Use This

This is a manual you run, not a book you finish. If you read it end to end and change nothing, that's not unusual — it's just not what the book is for. Come back to any chapter and try one tool; that's the point where it starts working.

It is built on one distinction. Knowing why a pattern exists is mostly declarative knowledge — facts and explanations — and it updates when something is explained well. Being able to move differently inside that pattern behaves more like a skill, and it updates mainly through repetition. You almost certainly have the first already. Every tool here targets the second.

### Who This Is For

You've read the books. You can name your patterns, sometimes in their clinical vocabulary. None of it has changed what happens on a Tuesday morning when the work is open and you don't begin. If that's you, whether the problem is low mood, avoidance, stalling, a standard nothing clears, or a voice that is reliably vicious, this is for you.

If you are in crisis, or your symptoms are severe enough that daily life is coming apart, this book is not enough on its own. Chapter 00 shows you how to tell.

### THREE OPERATING RULES

1. **One tool at a time, for at least a week.**
   Collecting techniques is the same avoidance as researching them.
2. **Written down, not done in your head.**
   Most of these exercises lose their effect when done mentally. The writing does the work; it isn't just a record of it. If physical writing is blocked, use voice memos or digital dictation. The drill works the same.
3. **Five minutes counts.**
   A badly done exercise beats a skipped one, permanently and by a wide margin.

Measure, don't guess. Feelings are a poor gauge of progress when mood is low, because low mood edits the memory of the week. The two-minute weekly check-in in Chapter 00 tells you whether this is working. Run it from the first week.

What this is not. This is psychoeducation and structured self-help. It is not therapy, and it is not a substitute for a clinician. The methods here come mainly from behavioural activation and cognitive behavioural therapy. Both have reasonable evidence as self-help, and both work better with some professional support than without it. [I] If you can get care at any point, this book becomes a supplement to it rather than a replacement.

A note on the worksheets. The fillable fields in the digital edition don't save anything. That's deliberate: this is private material. Print Appendix C, use a notebook, or use whatever's actually private for you — a notes app only you open, for instance. Choose what keeps it yours.

*That's the operating manual for the manual. Nothing after this needs to be read in order, and nothing expires. Put it down whenever you want and open it at whichever chapter matches the day.*

# CHAPTER 00
## Know Your Starting Point

*Rule out what a book can't fix, set a baseline, and decide in advance when to get more help.*

Every tool in this book assumes the machinery is basically sound and currently jammed. That is usually true, but not always. Ten minutes here makes sure you aren't pushing a behavioural solution against a problem that isn't behavioural.

### Check the Body First
Low mood, exhaustion and difficulty starting can be driven or worsened by physical factors. If you can, ask a doctor about:

*   **Thyroid function, iron and anaemia, vitamin B12 and vitamin D.** These are common, simple to test, and easy to overlook.
*   **Sleep.** This includes insomnia, irregular sleep timing, and sleep apnoea (snoring, gasping, waking unrefreshed).
*   **Medication side effects,** including from medicines that have nothing to do with mood.
*   **Alcohol, cannabis and other substances.** These are often used to cope and often make low mood worse.
*   **Hormonal changes,** including after pregnancy and around menopause, and chronic pain or illness.

If you take any medication, don't change or stop it because of anything in this book. That conversation belongs with your prescriber (the doctor or nurse who prescribed it).

### Two Patterns Worth Assessing Specifically

**ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder).** Consider an assessment if difficulty starting, finishing and organising has been with you since childhood, is present even when your mood is fine, and shows up across work, home and relationships. The grading tools here help, but assessment and treatment can change what's possible in ways that practice alone can't.

**Bipolar patterns.** Mention it to a clinician (a doctor, psychiatrist or therapist) if you've ever had stretches of several days with unusually high or irritable mood, much less need for sleep, racing thoughts, or risk-taking that was out of character for you. If this applies, keep sleep and daily routine regular above all, and increase activity with professional guidance rather than on your own.

### Set a Baseline: The Weekly Check-In

Same day each week, two minutes, written down. Record five numbers:
*   **Mood:** your average for the week, 0-10.
*   **Floor days:** how many days you met your minimum viable day — see Chapter 08 for what that means; until you've written yours, estimate or leave this blank. 0-7.
*   **Blocks started:** scheduled blocks — the specific times you plan an activity for, see Chapter 02 — started out of scheduled blocks planned. Started counts; finished doesn't matter here.
*   **Sleep:** average hours, and whether your timing was roughly regular. On rotating shifts or nights, "regular" means regular for your own pattern, not necessarily daytime hours.
*   **One line:** the most notable thing about the week.

A blank tracker is in Appendix C. If you want a standardised measure as well, the PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9) is a free, widely used nine-question depression questionnaire. Many doctors use it, and it's easy to find from reputable health services. [0] Take it at the start and every two weeks. Treat the scores as a signal for a conversation with a professional, not as a diagnosis you give yourself.

**Its last question asks about thoughts of being better off dead, or of hurting yourself.** If you answer anything above "not at all" on that question, go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now) today — whatever your total score is. That question doesn't wait two weeks.

### Decide Now: When Self-Help Isn't Enough

Set your escalation line while you can think clearly. On the day you need it, you'll be inclined to argue that things aren't bad enough yet.

| IF THIS IS TRUE | DO THIS |
| :--- | :--- |
| Thoughts of death, suicide or self-harm | [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now), today |
| Low mood or loss of interest most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, and it's affecting work, relationships or basic self-care | Book an appointment with a doctor or mental health professional this week. Keep using the book alongside it. |
| Four to six weeks of consistent use, and the weekly check-in is flat or worse | Escalate: book that appointment with a doctor or mental health professional. This is information about the problem, not a verdict on your effort. |
| You can't manage the minimum viable day on most days for a week | Escalate: book that appointment with a doctor or mental health professional. |
| You're drinking or using more to get through the day | Escalate: book that appointment with a doctor or mental health professional, and tell them about the drinking or using specifically. |

### Care on a Budget
Care is sometimes more reachable than it looks. Options worth checking include your primary care doctor or GP, university or college counselling, employee assistance programmes (which are usually free and often unused), community and non-profit clinics, therapists who charge on a sliding scale, digital CBT programmes run by public health services, faith or community leaders you trust, and peer support groups. Appendix D covers how to use this book alongside professional help.

If cost isn't the barrier but confidentiality is — an employer who might learn about it, a residence status tied to your job — that's a real constraint, not a reason to rule out care. Ask what a service can promise about privacy before you rule it out, and look for options that sit outside your workplace, such as a community clinic or a therapist you find and pay for directly.

**TRY THIS TODAY**
Write your first weekly check-in now. Then write, in one sentence, the point at which you will contact a professional. That sentence is the whole assignment.

*This is the least exciting chapter and one of the most important. If you've written your baseline and your escalation line, you've done more than most readers of most books ever do. Stop here if you want.*

# CHAPTER 01
## Action Comes First

*Stop waiting for the feeling. Start, and let the feeling catch up.*

Action usually comes first. Motivation tends to follow.

The intuition almost everyone runs on is: feel like doing the thing, then do the thing. Under low mood, that sequence tends to reverse. Motivation stops arriving in advance and starts arriving during the task. Sometimes that's a few minutes in. Sometimes it takes days of repetition. Either way, it arrives only if you started without it.

Waiting for the feeling is therefore not patience. It is waiting for an output while withholding the input.

### Why the Loop Tightens
Low mood reduces activity. Reduced activity removes the sources of reward, contact and competence that would lift mood. Mood drops further, and activity drops further. The loop reinforces itself, and it doesn't need a character flaw to run. The mechanism is enough on its own.

> **Low Mood / Exhaustion** -> **Do Less / Retreat** -> **Fewer Rewards (Less contact, less lift)** -> **Self-Trust Diminishes**

The loop runs on its own. Activity is the only box you can change by deciding to.

### Where the Loop Is Breakable
Not at mood, because you can't decide to feel different. Not at the thoughts either, at least not first; they are downstream, and they argue back. The intervention point is **activity**, because it is the only part of the loop you can change directly by deciding to.

That is the logic of behavioural activation. In major trials for depression it has performed comparably to cognitive therapy, and it is considerably simpler to run yourself. [1]

The practical consequence: from here on, "I don't feel like it" stops being information about whether to act. It is a weather report. It describes conditions, not instructions.

### When the Lift Doesn't Come
Sometimes you do the thing and feel nothing. That isn't evidence the method failed. Reward tends to register faintly in a system that hasn't been used for a while, and in heavier depression the lift can lag by weeks. Two adjustments help:

*   **Score accomplishment, not only pleasure.** A sense of having done something usually returns before enjoyment does.
*   **Judge the trend over two to three weeks, not the day.** One flat afternoon tells you nothing. A flat month tells you something, and that goes to the escalation table in Chapter 00, not to a verdict about you.

**TRY THIS TODAY**
Pick one thing you've been waiting to feel like doing. Do the first two minutes of it without the feeling. Afterwards, note whether anything shifted, even slightly.

*That's the mechanism the whole book runs on, and it fits in one sentence. If you read nothing else today, this chapter has already done most of its work. Close the book and let it sit.*

# CHAPTER 02
## The Activation Protocol

*Monitor, build a menu, schedule by the clock, and grade everything down until it can be started.*

The protocol has three stages. Most people skip to stage three, schedule something ambitious, fail at it, and conclude the method doesn't work. Do them in order.

### Stage 1 - Monitor for Three Days
Before changing anything, record what you actually do. Each hour, write one line: what you did, and three ratings out of 10. **P** is how much pleasure it gave. **A** is how much sense of accomplishment it gave. **Mood** is your mood at that point — this is what lets you spot, afterwards, when it was lowest.

| TIME | WHAT I DID | P | A | MOOD |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 09:00 | Scrolled in bed | 2 | 0 | 3 |
| 10:00 | Made food, ate properly | 4 | 3 | 4 |
| 11:00 | Read about a new framework | 3 | 1 | 4 |
| 12:00 | Short walk to the shop | 4 | 2 | 5 |

Hourly is ideal. If hourly won't happen, three check-ins spread across your day are fine — for a standard day that might be late morning, afternoon and evening; on a rotating or night shift, pick three points that fit your own pattern instead. A rough log you keep beats a perfect one you abandon on day one. Three days is enough. Look for three things:
*   Activities that score above 4 in either column.
*   How much of the day goes to things scoring 0-2 in both.
*   When mood is lowest, and what you were doing just before.

### Stage 2 - Build the Menu
From the log, write three lists. Keep them short.
*   **Reliable P:** things that scored on pleasure, however small or silly.
*   **Reliable A:** things that scored on accomplishment, however trivial.
*   **Dead time:** the 0-2 activities that take up the most hours.

Add one more item to the P and A lists: *something you used to value that has quietly dropped away*. A friend you stopped messaging, a walk you stopped taking, an instrument in a cupboard. Low mood tends to prune these first, and they are often the most restorative things to bring back.

### Stage 3 - Schedule by the Clock, Not by Mood
Put items from the first two lists into specific hours, in advance. This is not a to-do list. A to-do list is decided at the moment of doing, which is when you have the least capacity. A schedule is decided beforehand, and that is the entire point.

Write each item as an if-then plan: "When [time or cue], I will [small action] at [place]." For example: "When I finish my morning coffee, I will open the laptop at the kitchen table and write one sentence." Plans in this form are reliably more likely to be carried out than intentions alone. [2]

Balance the menu. Schedule at least one P item every day, not only chores. A week built entirely of obligations is a week the loop will win.

> **If you noted a bipolar pattern in Chapter 00:** increase your scheduled activity gradually and with professional guidance, not by ramping this protocol up on your own — and keep your sleep and daily routine as regular as you can while you do.

### The Grading Rule (The Active Ingredient)
"Grading" here just means cutting a task down in size — nothing to do with school marks. Every scheduled item must be startable on your worst day, not your average one. "Sort out the paperwork" won't get started. "Open the top envelope" will.

| TOO BIG (TRIGGERS AVOIDANCE) | GRADED DOWN TO STARTABLE |
| :--- | :--- |
| Go to the gym | Put gym clothes on |
| Sort out the paperwork | Open one envelope |
| Clean the flat | Clear one surface |
| Apply for jobs | Open the job site and read one listing |
| Write the landing page | Write one sentence of it |
| Reply to everything | Reply to one message |
| Get back in touch with people | Send one person a one-line message |

An absurdly small task completed beats a reasonable one skipped. Completion is the active ingredient, not size. Once a small version is happening reliably, grade it back up one step at a time.

**IF IT'S NOT WORKING**
*   You keep skipping the log. Drop to three check-ins a day. Partial data is still data.
*   Things are scheduled but not started. They're graded wrong. Go smaller, and see Chapter 05.
*   Things get done but there's no lift. Read "When the Lift Doesn't Come" in Chapter 01. Track the A column, and judge the trend over weeks.
*   The schedule is all obligations. Add one P item a day, non-negotiable.

# CHAPTER 03
## Interrogating the Failure Thought

*Not positive thinking. Accurate thinking, checked on paper.*

Some thoughts arrive fully formed and feel like observations rather than opinions. They never get examined, because they don't announce themselves as claims. "I've wasted years." "Everyone my age is ahead." "I never finish anything." They present as facts about reality. They are actually predictions and evaluations, and both can be tested.

The method is not positive thinking, which rarely works and which you'd probably reject anyway. It is treating the thought as a claim and checking it against evidence, the way you'd check any other claim. [3]

### Common Shapes
Distorted thoughts tend to follow a small number of patterns. Naming the pattern is often half the work.

| SHAPE | SOUNDS LIKE |
| :--- | :--- |
| All-or-nothing | "If it isn't excellent, it's worthless." |
| Overgeneralising | "I never finish anything." |
| Mind reading | "They can tell there's something wrong with me." |
| Fortune telling | "This will fail like the last one." |
| Discounting | "That one worked out, but it doesn't count." |
| Labelling | "I'm a failure," instead of "this attempt failed." |

### The Thought Record (Written, Not Mental)

| COLUMN | WHAT GOES IN IT |
| :--- | :--- |
| Situation | What was happening, factually, in one line |
| Emotion | What you felt, named in one word, rated 0-100 |
| Thought | The exact sentence, in the words it arrived in |
| Belief | How true it feels right now, 0-100% |
| Evidence for | Facts that genuinely support it. Be fair here. |
| Evidence against | Facts that don't fit it, including ones you'd normally discount |

Then write one sentence that accounts for both evidence columns, and re-rate both the belief and the emotion. The emotion rating is the one that actually matters. The belief is how you get to it.

**WORKED EXAMPLE ONE — THE AUTHOR'S OWN**
*   **Situation:** Sat down to work at 09:45. Still sitting there at 11:00, having done nothing.
*   **Emotion:** Shame, 80
*   **Thought:** "I never finish anything. That's just what I am."
*   **Belief:** 90%
*   **Evidence for:** Two products abandoned or stalled. No public launch to date. Several restarts from zero.
*   **Evidence against:** One app has a dozen features built and deployed. A second product is live, with a brand document and positioning. A university degree, finished. Both stalls had identifiable practical causes, not a loss of ability. The pattern is stalling at a specific point, not a general inability to finish.
*   **Balanced sentence:** "I stall at the point where a project needs money or an outside party, and I haven't yet pushed through that point. That's a specific, fixable failure mode, not a description of my character."
*   **Re-rate:** Belief 65%. Shame 55.

**WORKED EXAMPLE TWO — SALARIED, STUCK IN PLACE**
*   **Situation:** Six years in the same role. A colleague two years behind me was promoted past me last month.
*   **Emotion:** Shame, 70
*   **Thought:** "I've wasted years here. Everyone my age is ahead of me."
*   **Belief:** 80%
*   **Evidence for:** Six years without a promotion. One colleague promoted past me. No move of my own in that time.
*   **Evidence against:** I changed teams twice and picked up skills neither of my past roles used. My manager has twice said, outside any review, that my technical work is the strongest on the team. "Everyone my age" turns out to be a handful of people I compare myself to, not everyone; two of them have told me they'd swap for my job. Comparing years served to one person's promotion ignores what the years were actually for.
*   **Balanced sentence:** "I haven't been promoted in six years, and I've also built skills nobody's asked me to use yet. Both are true, and neither one makes the years 'wasted.'"
*   **Re-rate:** Belief 65%. Shame 55.

**WORKED EXAMPLE THREE — DOMESTIC ACCUMULATION**
*   **Situation:** Stood in the kitchen at 10:00 on Saturday looking at two weeks of unwashed dishes, unopened post, and laundry piles.
*   **Emotion:** Overwhelm, 85
*   **Thought:** "I can't run a basic adult life. I've let everything slide completely."
*   **Belief:** 90%
*   **Evidence for:** The flat is disordered. Bills are unopened. I haven't cooked a proper meal in ten days.
*   **Evidence against:** I worked 50 hours this week during a team restructuring and had a fever on Tuesday. The flat was functional three weeks ago. Mess accumulates when energy drops; it doesn't indicate permanent incompetence. Clearing the sink takes twelve minutes, not three weeks.
*   **Balanced sentence:** "The flat is disordered because I hit physical capacity during an intense fortnight, not because I am incapable of running a home."
*   **Re-rate:** Belief 50%. Overwhelm 40.

**WORKED EXAMPLE FOUR — ADMINISTRATIVE DEBT**
*   **Situation:** Three overdue tax and insurance notices sitting unread on the desk for a month.
*   **Emotion:** Dread, 80
*   **Thought:** "I've ruined my finances permanently and I'm going to face catastrophic penalties."
*   **Belief:** 85%
*   **Evidence for:** The deadlines passed. Late fees will apply. I ignored two reminders.
*   **Evidence against:** The underlying accounts have sufficient funds to clear the original balance and standard late fees. No legal action has been initiated. Two of the letters are automated notices, not final demands. Stalling generates more dread than the numbers on the page ever do.
*   **Balanced sentence:** "I incurred late fees by avoiding the post, but the problem is finite, quantified, and solvable the moment envelope one is open."
*   **Re-rate:** Belief 55%. Dread 35.

The shape is identical across all four examples. The situations differ, but the mechanism doesn't: an over-general claim, once tested, comes back narrower and more accurate. It never comes back comfortable, just more correct.

Calibrate your expectations. A drop of 10 to 30 points on the emotion rating is a good result — that's the rating that actually matters (see above). You aren't trying to reach zero. You're trying to get the thought down to the size the evidence supports. Some records will barely move, especially early on or on the worst days. Those still count as practice, and the effect builds with repetition. Two of the examples above (Three and Four) moved further than this range. That's not the typical case, and yours doesn't need to match theirs to have worked.

When not to use this. If the thought is accurate ("I'm two months behind on rent"), it doesn't need challenging. It needs a decision: what's the one next step you can take today, however small? Testing true thoughts only produces frustration. If the thought is about wanting to die, or about hurting yourself, this tool isn't the right one either — go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now) instead of writing it down alone.

> **Stop if:** writing the thought down makes it louder, brings up thoughts of dying or hurting yourself, or your distress spikes sharply. Put the record down and go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now). If you're not sure whether a thought needs this record or a person, use a person.

### FIELD DRILL: THOUGHT RECORD
1.  **SITUATION (FACTS ONLY)**
    Who, what, where, when
2.  **EMOTION AND INTENSITY (0-100)**
    e.g. shame, 80
3.  **THE THOUGHT, WORD FOR WORD**
    The exact sentence in your head
4.  **BELIEF (0-100%)**
    e.g. 90
5.  **EVIDENCE FOR (FACTS ONLY)**
    Facts that support the thought
6.  **EVIDENCE AGAINST (INCLUDING WHAT YOU'D NORMALLY DISCOUNT)**
    Facts that don't fit
7.  **BALANCED SENTENCE**
    One sentence that accounts for both columns
8.  **RE-RATE BELIEF AND EMOTION**
    e.g. belief 65%, shame 55

*That's the tool. It takes about ten minutes on paper, and it is genuinely tiring the first few times. That's normal, and not a sign you're doing it wrong. A good place to stop.*

# CHAPTER 04
## Calibrating the Standard

*High standards improve work. Impossible ones prevent it. Here's how to tell them apart and test them.*

High standards improve work. Impossible standards prevent it. They are easy to confuse because they use the same vocabulary, and because the second one feels more virtuous than the first. Clinically, the problem isn't having high standards. It's when your sense of self-worth depends on meeting them, and you keep them in place despite the obvious costs. [4]

### The Test (Four Questions)
1.  **Has anything you've made ever cleared it?** If nothing ever has, it isn't calibrated to reality.
2.  **Would you apply it to someone else's first attempt?** If not, it isn't a standard. It's a rule with one target.
3.  **Does it tell you what to do, or only what's insufficient?** Usable standards say what to do. Impossible ones only veto.
4.  **When you meet it, does it stay met?** Or does the bar move up so the verdict stays the same?

A standard that fails questions 1 and 4 is not measuring your work. It is producing a verdict decided in advance, and the work is a formality.

### Where These Can Come From
For some people, standards like this were shaped early, calibrated against approval that was scarce or depended on performance. If that fits you, it may explain why meeting the bar produces relief rather than satisfaction, and why the relief doesn't last. If it doesn't fit, that's fine too. You don't need an origin story for any of this to work. The tools target what the standard does now, not where it came from, and arguing with its history rarely loosens it.

### Scope It Instead
Don't attack the standard. Demote it from a veto on each individual item to a rule for the portfolio as a whole.

| APPLIED AS | REAL-WORLD EFFECT |
| :--- | :--- |
| Every project must clear it, before starting | Nothing starts. The standard becomes a gate at the entrance. |
| The body of work should clear it, over years | Things get made. Some are practice. The standard becomes a direction. |

The second framing doesn't lower your ambition. It's the only version in which ambition can come out as output rather than paralysis.

### Run One Experiment
Arguing with a standard changes little. Testing it can. [4] Deliberately ship (put out into the world) one thing at 80%: a message without the third reread, a draft without the final polish, a post without the last round of edits.

> **Stop if:** the standard you're testing covers safety-critical work — medication doses, sign-offs, legal filings, anything where getting it wrong could hurt someone. Don't run the 80% experiment there. And if your checking has the repetitive, can't-stop quality of OCD rather than ordinary perfectionism, this chapter isn't the right tool on its own — talk to a doctor or therapist about it directly.

1.  **Before:** write down what you predict will happen, and how sure you are, 0-100%.
2.  **Do it.** Don't fix it afterwards.
3.  **After:** write down what actually happened. Compare it with the prediction.

Most people find the feared consequence either didn't happen or was much smaller than predicted. Each result is a data point the standard has to account for, and it can't absorb these the way it absorbs arguments.

The practical move: pick your current project. Decide once, in writing, whether it's a bar-clearing project or a rep — a practice attempt, not a final verdict. Then hold that decision. If it's a rep, the bar doesn't get to vote on it again. Re-opening the question mid-task is the thing to notice and stop, not the thing to settle.

**TRY THIS TODAY**
Choose one small 80% experiment. Write down your prediction. Run it within 24 hours.

# CHAPTER 05
## The Not-Starting Moment

*The resistance is attached to starting, not to the task. Get past the start.*

There is a specific instant when the work is scheduled, you are physically present, and you don't begin. Everything else in this manual is preparation for that instant.

What it actually feels like. Not laziness; laziness is comfortable, and this isn't. It's rising pressure in the chest or gut, a sudden conviction that you need to check something first, a strong pull toward any other screen. It is aversion, and it behaves like other urges: it rises, peaks and usually fades within minutes, whether or not you act on it.

Research on procrastination describes it as short-term mood repair. Avoiding the task makes the bad feeling drop immediately, and that immediate relief is exactly what gets reinforced. [5] Each escape teaches your nervous system that the task really was a threat.

### Urge Surfing, Applied to Avoidance
Urge surfing comes from relapse-prevention work on cravings. [5] Here it's adapted: instead of escaping the feeling, you let it run without obeying it.

1.  **Name it:** "This is avoidance, not a decision."
2.  **Locate it in your body:** chest, throat or stomach. Find where it actually sits.
3.  **Don't act for ninety seconds.** Don't argue with it, don't start the task, don't escape. Just watch it.
4.  **Notice it shift.** It usually peaks and starts to ease. That is the thing you're proving: the feeling and the behaviour can be separated.
5.  **Then take the smallest possible step toward the task.** Surfing is the on-ramp, not the destination. Watching the urge indefinitely is just a more mindful way of not starting.

> **Check your body first.** New or unusual chest pressure — especially with shortness of breath, pain spreading to your arm or jaw, or a racing heart that doesn't fit the moment — needs a doctor before it needs this chapter. See Chapter 00, Check the Body First. Once that's ruled out, treat it as avoidance, not before.

> **Stop if:** ninety seconds of watching the feeling makes it climb rather than ease, or it tips into panic. That's not a sign you're doing it wrong — some bodies do this, especially with a history of panic or trauma. Stop watching, do something grounding instead (feet on the floor, cold water on your hands, naming five things you can see), and if it keeps happening, don't do this exercise alone.

### The Five-Minute Rule
Commit to five minutes of the task, with explicit, genuine permission to stop at the end. Set a timer.

This is not a trick to get you to work for an hour. If it becomes one, it stops working, because you'll learn the permission was fake. Stop at five minutes when you want to. That's what keeps the mechanism honest.

Why it works: the cost of starting is almost entirely at the front. The aversion is attached to the transition, not the activity. A couple of minutes in, the thing generating the resistance has usually gone.

If five minutes is too much, the task is graded wrong. Cut it down until the first step is something like opening the file, finding the form, or standing up. No size is too small to be worth doing. A badly graded task you avoid teaches avoidance, and a trivial one you complete teaches completion. Only one of those builds in your favour.

### Pre-Decide the Hard Moment
Write one if-then plan for the exact moment you tend to escape: "If I notice myself reaching for my phone at the start of a block, then I put it in another room and start the five-minute timer." A decision made in advance doesn't have to be made under pressure.

**TRY THIS TODAY**
At your next scheduled block: name it, ninety seconds, smallest step, five-minute timer. Write down what happened.

*This is the chapter you'll use most, and you now have enough to get through tomorrow morning. Close the book here with a clear conscience. Everything after this is refinement.*

# CHAPTER 06
## Values Over Goals

*A goal is a destination. A value is a direction you can move in today.*

Goals are achieved and then gone. Values are directions you can move in today, whatever the outcome. [6] The distinction sounds soft, but it does real work: on a day when no goal feels reachable, a value still tells you what to do.

| DIMENSION | GOAL (DESTINATION) | VALUE (DIRECTION) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Example | Get the flat sorted by Sunday | Live somewhere that isn't working against me |
| Example | Find a job by March | Do work that uses what I'm good at |
| Status | Achieved or failed | Ongoing; can't be failed |
| On a bad day | Feels impossibly far away | Still actionable in five minutes |
| When reached | Gone, and needs replacing | Unchanged; keeps directing |

Why this matters. When self-worth is loaded onto finishing goals, every unfinished thing becomes evidence about you as a person. That makes starting expensive, because starting risks a verdict. Acting from a value takes the verdict out of the transaction. You aren't proving anything today; you're pointed in a direction today.

### Finding Yours: Two Questions
First: think of a time you were absorbed in something and lost track of time. Not a proud memory; an absorbed one. What was the activity actually doing? Solving a puzzle? Making something exist that didn't before? Understanding a system all the way down?

Second: what irritates you in other people's work? Irritation at sloppiness points to a value of craft. Irritation at things that shut people out points to a value of access. Your annoyances are often a more honest signal than your aspirations.

Write three values, no more, in your own words. The should-check: if a value sounds like something you'd say to impress someone, or something you think you ought to care about, it's probably a "should", not a value. Cross it out.

# CHAPTER 07
## The Rumination Trap

*If a decision or action wouldn't end it, it isn't thinking. It's circling.*

Rumination — going round in circles in your head — is the most convincing form of avoidance there is, because it looks exactly like thinking hard about the problem. It feels productive, and it reliably produces nothing. Research on repetitive thinking suggests the key difference isn't how much you think but how: abstract "why" questions keep the loop going, while concrete "what" and "how" questions tend to end it. [7]

### The Test That Separates Them
One question: would a decision or an action end this?

| PROBLEM-SOLVING | RUMINATION |
| :--- | :--- |
| About something that can change | About something already fixed, or abstract |
| "What do I do about X?" | "Why am I like this?" |
| Ends in an action | Ends when you get tired |
| Feels like narrowing down | Feels like circling |
| Ten minutes usually does it | Forty minutes, and you're further back |

If the same thought has come round twice with no new information, it's rumination. A third pass confirms it.

### Three Interrupts (Strongest First)
1.  **Go concrete.** Rewrite the loop as a specific question with a possible answer. "Why am I always behind?" doesn't convert. "What exactly happened this week, and what's the one thing blocking Monday?" does. Answer it in writing, pick the next step, and stop. If a loop won't convert at all, it isn't a problem, it's a mood, and moods respond to activity, not analysis. Go to Chapter 02. This doesn't apply to a loop about wanting to die or to hurt yourself — that isn't a mood to wait out. Go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now) instead.
2.  **Name it and move.** Say "circling", then change your body. Stand up, walk to another room, go outside, put something on. The state is partly physical, and changing posture interrupts it more reliably than arguing with what it's saying.
3.  **Postpone it.** Set fifteen minutes at the same point in your day, every day — on a rotating or shift pattern, pick a fixed point in your own cycle rather than a clock time. When the loop starts outside that window, write the topic on a list and stop. At the appointed time, sit down and think about the list on purpose. Many items have lost their pull by then, which is a lesson in itself. This technique was developed for anxious worry, and it's a useful backup for depressive loops. [7] Don't put thoughts of dying, hurting yourself, or a trauma memory on this list to think about later — those go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now) or a professional now, not onto a schedule.

> **Stop if:** what's circling is a thought about dying, hurting yourself, or a memory that's re-living rather than reviewing. None of the three interrupts above are the right tool for that. Go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now), or talk to a doctor or therapist about it directly.

### The Trap for People Who Read About Psychology
Understanding your own mechanisms is genuinely useful. It is also the best fuel rumination can get. Analysing a pattern can stand in for changing it indefinitely, and it feels like progress the whole time.

The check: has this analysis changed anything I did this week? If not, it's the loop wearing a lab coat.

# CHAPTER 08
## The Bad-Day Protocol

*Define the floor in advance. Never let a gap reach two days.*

Decide this now, while you're able to decide things. On the day you need it, you won't be able to design it.

Write your minimum viable day: three items. They aren't work items. They're the floor below which the day counts as lost. Something like: get up and dressed, eat one real meal, go outside once. That's the whole bar. Hitting three tiny things on a bad day is a completed day, not a failed one.

### The Rule That Matters: Never Miss Twice
A missed day means a day you didn't hit your minimum viable day at all — not a day you did a smaller version, and not a day covered by "a genuinely bad stretch" below.

One missed day is an event. Two in a row make a third easier to miss, and the longer a gap runs the more it costs to restart. So the target isn't perfection. It's making sure a gap doesn't run past one day if you can help it.

One well-known study found that missing a single opportunity didn't meaningfully set back how habits formed. [8] That study is about one miss, not about what happens after two or three — this rule is the book's own judgement about momentum, not something the study measured directly. What matters, either way, is coming back, not keeping an unbroken streak.

```text
               ┌── Resume at Minimum ───> Gap Closed
Missed Day 1 ──┤
               └── Miss Again (Day 2) ──> Pattern Forms
```

### Resuming Without the Restart Ritual
After a gap, the temptation is to relaunch: a new plan, a clean slate, a bigger commitment to make up for lost time. That's the collapse-and-restart cycle, and the oversized recommitment is what guarantees the next collapse.

Instead, resume at the minimum. Do one block, graded down small, on the next scheduled day. Nothing is rebuilt, nothing is made up for, and the plan isn't revised. You just rejoin it.

### What a Gap Is Actually Telling You

| THE GAP CAME FROM | THE FIX |
| :--- | :--- |
| Blocks too big to start | Grade them down further |
| Blocks that don't connect to anything you care about | Check them against your values (Chapter 06) |
| A genuinely bad stretch: low mood, illness, a hard week | Nothing structural. Run the minimum day and wait it out — this is real permission, not a loophole. If the stretch doesn't lift, use the escalation line in Chapter 00 alongside it; waiting it out and escalating aren't opposites. |

People most often get the third case wrong. Sometimes the answer is that the week was bad and no change to the system is needed. Redesigning the system after every bad week is how you end up with a system you've never actually run.

The escalation line. If the minimum viable day itself is out of reach on most days for a week, that's past what this chapter covers. Use the escalation table in Chapter 00.

# CHAPTER 09
## Self-Attack as a Defence

*Self-loathing often isn't a conclusion. It's a move, and moves can be changed.*

This chapter is about the critical voice in your own head — the one that argues, judges and won't let up. If you hear a voice, or voices, that don't feel like your own thinking, that's a different thing, and worth mentioning to a doctor rather than working through here.

If the self-attack has moved from words to actions, such as hurting yourself, or you're thinking about it, this chapter isn't the tool right now. Go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now) — it covers this, not only suicidal crisis. Come back to this chapter once you're safe.

> **Don't do this alone if** the voice in your head echoes, closely, someone who was cruel, controlling or abusive to you. The exercises below ask you to look straight at it, and that's a heavier thing when the voice has a real source. Do it with a therapist if you can, not solo, and not for the first time on a hard day.

Self-loathing is not usually a symptom sitting there doing nothing. Often it's a behaviour, kept going because it works. Looking at what it does for you (its function) is more useful than debating what it says.

What it buys you. Three things, all real, all delivered immediately:
*   **Pre-emption.** If you deliver the verdict first, nobody else's can land as hard. You can't be caught out by a judgement you've already made.
*   **Avoiding the test.** As long as the question is settled, you never have to run the experiment that might answer it badly. Conceding the point protects you from finding out.
*   **The feel of integrity.** Self-criticism is socially coded as honesty, humility and accountability. It's the only form of avoidance that feels like a virtue from the inside.

That's a strong reward: immediate relief, a delayed cost, and moral cover. Anything rewarded like that gets repeated often, and none of it requires you to be weak or self-indulgent. It only requires the mechanism to work, which it does.

Why arguing with it fails. You can't refute it, because it isn't a conclusion. It's a move. Counter-evidence gets absorbed ("that doesn't count"), because its job is protection, not accuracy. That's why years of reading haven't dented it. You've been debating something that was never making an argument.

### The Tool: Cost-Benefit, Honestly Filled
Do this for the behaviour, not for yourself. Fill in both columns properly; a rigged one teaches nothing. The table below is a worked example, to show the shape of it — not a verdict on you. Use it as a template, and write your own entries underneath in your own words.

| BENEFITS OF SELF-ATTACK | COSTS OF SELF-ATTACK |
| :--- | :--- |
| Nobody can judge me worse than I already have | Nothing I make can ever count; the verdict is pre-written |
| I never risk trying and being found wanting | Avoiding the test means the question stays open forever |
| It feels rigorous and honest | It can't be proven wrong, which is the opposite of rigour |
| It's familiar; I know how to do this | It keeps costing time the protection was supposed to save — not a verdict on the years already gone, just a reason to stop paying now |

_________________________________________________________________
_________________________________________________________________

Read the right-hand column as a price list, because that's what it is. The protection isn't free, and it isn't cheap.

### Three Signs It's Defence, Not Assessment
1.  **It's global.** "Incompetent in every way." Real assessment is specific and names a domain.
2.  **It arrives before the evidence.** Verdict first, then a search for support.
3.  **It never updates.** Nothing you could produce would change it, which means it isn't tracking your output at all.

### Changing the Response in the Moment
This isn't positive self-talk. You'd reject that, correctly, because it's false and obviously a technique. Four steps instead:

1.  **Label the move, not the content.** Say "That's the defence," not "that's not true." You're naming what it does, and that's much harder for it to absorb.
2.  **Delay; don't debate.** Give it ten minutes before engaging. Urges fade, and most of the time the conversation never resumes.
3.  **Replace it with something specific and true.** Not "I'm capable." Something narrow enough to be accurate: "I don't know yet whether I can finish this. I haven't run the test." Uncertainty is honest, and it leaves the question open, which is exactly what the defence exists to prevent.
4.  **Change the tone, not only the words.** Say the accurate sentence the way a decent coach would say it to someone they're backing: just as honest, without the contempt. People tend to skip this step, and it matters. In compassion-focused approaches, the tone of the inner voice is a target in its own right, because the same accurate sentence lands differently when it isn't delivered as an attack. [9]

If warmth feels impossible, try distance instead. Write the sentence about yourself in the third person, using your own name, the way you'd write feedback for a colleague. Talking to yourself this way tends to make hard feedback easier to hear without flinching. [9]

The point isn't to feel good about yourself. It's to stop closing the question in advance, so that evidence can finally start to land on it. You don't have to win the argument. You have to stop having it long enough to ship something.

> **Stop if:** this is bringing up thoughts of dying, hurting yourself, or a memory rather than a thought. Put it down and go to [Read This First](#read-this-first-if-youre-not-safe-right-now), or talk to a doctor or therapist about it directly. That's not failing at this chapter — it's using it correctly.

*This is the deepest chapter in the book, and it doesn't need to land in one reading. If it's hit hard, that's worth telling someone — a friend, or a therapist; compassion-focused therapy (Appendix D) is built for exactly this. Put it down for now if you need to. You'll be better placed to use it later, and none of this has to stay settled tonight.*

# CHAPTER 10
## Keeping It

*Getting better and staying better are different skills. This is the second one.*

Improvement is rarely a straight line. Low mood tends to come back through the same doors it left by, and usually quietly. The goal of this chapter is to spot it at the door rather than in the living room. [10]

**1. Your Early Warning Signs**
What slips first for you? Common ones:
*   Sleep timing drifts later, or sleep gets longer and less restful.
*   You stop replying to people, starting with the easy ones.
*   The log or the weekly check-in quietly stops.
*   Rumination time creeps up, often disguised as "planning".
*   The restart fantasy appears: a new system, starting Monday.

Look back at your own history and write down your top three. Be specific. "Low mood" is too late to be a warning sign. "Skipping my Sunday call" is early enough to act on.

**2. Your First Moves**
Decide in advance what you'll do when two warning signs appear in the same week. A good default:
1.  Go back to three days of monitoring (Chapter 02).
2.  Make the minimum viable day non-negotiable (Chapter 08).
3.  Make one real contact with one person, within 48 hours.

**3. Your Escalation Line**
Copy it from Chapter 00 and put it somewhere you'll see it. Name the specific person or service you'll contact, and how.

**4. Maintenance**
On good weeks, keep one tool running: the weekly check-in. It costs two minutes, and it's the instrument that catches the drift before you feel it.

# APPENDIX D
## Working With a Professional

This book works better alongside help than instead of it.

### Bring the Paperwork
Your activity logs, weekly check-ins and thought records are exactly what a clinician would otherwise spend sessions trying to reconstruct. Bring them. They shorten the assessment and make the first sessions far more useful.

### Approaches Worth Asking About
*   **Behavioural activation (BA)** - Part One of this book, delivered with guidance.
*   **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)** - Parts One and Two, plus much more.
*   **Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)** - the values work and urge surfing, in depth.
*   **Compassion-focused therapy (CFT)** - particularly relevant if Chapter 09 hit hard.
*   **Interpersonal therapy (IPT)** - if low mood is tied to relationships, loss or a major change in role.
*   **Medication** - a conversation with a prescriber, and often combined with therapy for moderate to severe depression.

### Questions to Ask
*   "What approach do you use, and what's the evidence for it with what I'm dealing with?"
*   "How will we know if it's working, and when would we change course?"
*   "What should I be doing between sessions?"

A good clinician will welcome these questions. If one doesn't, that tells you something too.

# CLOSING NOTE
## Starting from Jammed

The standing reminder: this manual is scaffolding for a problem it can't treat on its own. If care becomes reachable, whether a free service, a subsidised one or a clinician later on, take it, and let this become a supplement rather than a substitute.

And the last thing. Nothing in this book asks you to become a different person. Every tool in it assumes the machinery is sound and currently jammed, because that is usually what's true.

You don't have to believe that to use them. Procedures work whether or not you're convinced by them, which is exactly what makes them useful on the days you're convinced of nothing.

You are not starting from zero. You are starting from jammed, and there is a way out of jammed.

Pick one tool. Do it badly. That's the whole instruction.
